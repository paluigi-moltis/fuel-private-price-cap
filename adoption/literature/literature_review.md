# Literature review — cap adoption in retail fuel markets

Compiled 2026-10-06 from OpenAlex (title search, 60+ queries) and
Crossref (bibliographic verification of DOIs, volumes, pages). Raw
candidate pool: `candidates.json` (176 works); curated set: `selected.json`
(45 works); BibTeX: `references.bib`.

## 1. Dynamic oligopoly theory, kinked demand, Edgeworth cycles

- **Maskin & Tirole (1988)**, Econometrica 56(3):571-599 — game-theoretic
  foundations of kinked demand and Edgeworth cycles; multiple Markov
  equilibria. *Relevance:* frames why a coordinated move to a focal
  price (the cap) is one of the few equilibrium-selection devices that
  works without communication.
- **Noel (2007a)**, REStat 89(2):324-334 — Edgeworth cycles vs cost-based
  vs sticky pricing distinguished empirically. *Relevance:* our daily
  data show sticky-under-the-cap behaviour rather than cycling.
- **Noel (2007b)**, JIE 55(1):69-92 — Toronto evidence of cycles.
- **Eckert & West (2004)**, JLE 47(1):245-275 — cycle phase depends on
  station location. *Relevance:* supports spatial heterogeneity in
  adoption timing (our distance covariates).
- **Noel (2012)**, Energy Economics 34(3):824-833 — cycles as
  intertemporal price discrimination.
- **de Haas (2024)**, dissertation — German pump prices only weakly
  follow Edgeworth cycles; sticky pricing dominates.

## 2. Asymmetry, pass-through, local market power

- **Tappata (2009)**, RAND 40(3):468-495 — rockets-and-feathers from
  consumer search. *Relevance:* mechanism (search frictions) also
  predicts dispersion around the cap persists rather than collapses.
- **Deltas (2008)**, JIE 46(3):310-327 — retail prices respond faster
  to increases; local structure matters.
- **Verlinda (2008)**, JIE 56(3):581-612 — station-level asymmetry
  driven by local market power.
- **Bettendorf, van der Geest & Varkevisser (2003)**, Energy Economics
  25(6):669-689 — Dutch asymmetry.
- **Atil, Lahiani & Nguyen (2013)**, Energy Policy 63:1367-1376 —
  nonlinear pass-through.
- **Lewis (2011)**, JEMS 20(4):1109-1133 — asymmetric adjustment from
  search; *Relevance:* our design (exogenous focal cap) is immune to
  the cost-shock confounding that plagues this literature.

## 3. Market power, vertical structure, network oligopoly

- **Hastings & Gilbert (2005)**, JIE 46(4):545-561 — vertical
  integration raises wholesale prices to rivals.
- **Borenstein & Shepard (2002)**, RAND 33(2):315-337 — sticky prices
  and market power in wholesale gasoline.
- **Pellegrino (2025)**, AER — network approach to product
  differentiation and oligopoly markups. *Relevance:* network/production
  interpretation of why markups persist above administered caps.

## 4. Price controls: caps, floors, focal uniformity

- **Carranza, Clark & Houde (2015)**, JIE 83(1):46-63 — price floors
  reorganize market structure. *Relevance:* closest structural cousin —
  controls as market-organization devices.
- **Sen, Clemente & Jonker (2011)**, ALER 13(2):522-547 — Canadian
  gasoline ceilings: modest price effects, regulatory-capture concerns.
  *Relevance:* the only other station-level ceiling study with
  geographic detail; our setting is a *voluntary* cap (no legal force),
  cleanly separating focal-point from enforcement effects.
- **Zhang, Fei & Zheng (2020)**, Energy Economics 92:104950 — Chinese
  ceilings act as focal points producing uniformity. *Relevance:* our
  headline hypothesis; we observe the *diffusion path*, not just the
  uniformity outcome.
- **Quiguanas, Perez Cabrera & Carabalí (2025)**, JIBE — Colombian
  regulated gasoline: dispersion persists under ceilings.
- **Gatsios, Genakos & Papadokonstantaki (2026)**, SSRN WP — Greek
  daily-average ceiling: within-ceiling substitution, limited pass-on.
- **Contín-Pilart, Correljé & Huerta Arribas (1999)**, Energy Journal
  20(4) — Spain: ceiling → liberalization transition.
- **Braeutigam & Panzar (1993)**, AER P&P 83(2):163-168 and
  **Cabral & Riordan (1991)**, Springer chapter 147-166 — theory of
  incentive effects of price-cap regulation (utility context).

## 5. Consumer search and price dispersion

- **Reinganum (1979)**, JPE 87(5):979-1002 and **Stiglitz & Salop
  (1982)**, AER P&P 72(2):317-321 — canonical equilibrium dispersion
  models. *Relevance:* dispersion with identical agents is the null our
  bimodal result tests against.
- **Chandra & Tappata (2011)**, RAND 42(1):116-140 — temporal price
  dispersion tests in gasoline.
- **Haucap, Heimeshoff & Siekmann (2015)** — German station
  heterogeneity; **Yilmazkuday & Yilmazkuday (2016)**, Annals of
  Regional Science — dispersion decomposition.
- **Noel (2018)**, JIE 56(3):686-706 — natural experiment linking
  search shocks to dispersion.
- **Pennerstorfer, Schmidt-Dengler & Schutz (2020)**, IER 61(2):437-468
  — information and dispersion: inverted-U. *Relevance:* the cap acts
  as a public signal that shifts the information environment.

## 6. Spatial competition and the Italian fuel market

- **Rossi & Chintagunta (2015)**, JMR 52(5):669-684 — Italian highway
  price signs cut prices ~3% where visible. *Relevance:* Italian
  price-transparency evidence directly comparable to the cap as
  information shock.
- **Alderighi & Baudino (2015)**, Energy Economics — Cuneo market
  pricing behaviour.
- **Bergantino, Capozza & Intini (2020)**, Energy Economics 89:104876
  — spatial interaction, competition and territorial factors in
  Italian retail fuel. *Relevance:* validates our distance/territory
  covariate set for the Italian context.
- **Cardoso, Uchôa & Huamani (2021)**,PRS — new-rival spatial effects.
- **Mattioli, Dugato & Philips (2023)** — socio-spatial fuel-price
  vulnerability in Italy. *Relevance:* distributional stakes of our
  finding that non-adopter areas keep higher prices.
- **Albulescu & Mutascu (2021)**, Energy — FR/DE/IT price co-movement.

## 7. Price leadership and strategic complementarities

- **Atkinson, Eckert & West (2009)**, Economic Inquiry 47(4):669-685 —
  price matching and the domino effect. *Relevance:* direct precedent
  for domino-style sequential adoption; our hazard models quantify it.
- **Amir & Stepanova (2008)**, IJIO 26(3):579-594 — second-mover
  advantage in Bertrand. *Relevance:* explains why the cap initiator
  (Agip Eni) lost margin while followers timed entry.
- **Kim, Lan & Dobson (2019)**, OEP 71(4):1010-1036 — identifying
  leadership structures.
- **Amiti, Itskhoki & Konings (2020)**, AEJ:Macro 12(3):322-353 —
  direct estimates of strategic complementarities in price setting.
- **Guren (2018)**, JPE 126(6):2238-2318 — strategic complementarity
  generates price momentum (housing). *Relevance:* analog for our
  adoption momentum (19% → 51% in 8 days).
- **Manzano & Vives (2011)**, JME 48(6-7):1272-1295 — learning from
  prices, complementarity, multiplicity.

## 8. Duration methods for adoption

- **Jones & Branton (2005)**, SPPQ 6(4):419-430 — Cox duration models
  for policy adoption (method transfer to adoption events).
- **Burton, Rigby & Young (2003)**, AJARE 47(2):258-274 — duration
  analysis of technology adoption.
- **Sjoquist, Smith & Walker (2007)**, PB&F 27(2):225-245 — adoption
  timing with time-varying covariates.
- **Cox (1972)**, JRSS-B 34(2):187-220 — the model.

## Synthesis and gap

The literature establishes: (i) retail fuel prices are strategic
complements with local gradients (Deltas, Verlinda, Atkinson et al.);
(ii) administered prices can act as focal points producing uniformity
(Zhang et al.), with caveats on capture and dispersion (Sen et al.,
Quiguanas et al.); (iii) search frictions generate equilibrium
dispersion (Reinganum, Stiglitz-Salop, Chandra-Tappata).

**Gap filled by this paper:** no existing study observes the
*station-level diffusion path* of a *voluntary* cap at daily frequency
with communication-time precision. Previous ceiling studies compare
before/after aggregates; we identify who adopts, when, and why —
separating firm-network effects from spatial spillovers — and document
the reference-day measurement bias that official statistics incur when
a wave crosses a sampling date.
